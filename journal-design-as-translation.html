Design as Translation

On understanding what design actually is — not what it produces, but how it works.

For a long time, I never really asked myself what design was.

Of course, like every designer, I had read countless definitions. Design solves problems. Design communicates. Design creates experiences.

But knowing those definitions is not the same as truly understanding them.

I don't think I had genuinely experienced the meaning of design itself.

Perhaps that only comes with time. Perhaps it takes years of practice, mistakes, projects, conversations, and observations before the question begins to feel real.

The more interesting question

At some point, I became less interested in what design produces and more interested in what design actually is.

Not what design does, but how it works.

Because when we say that design solves problems, communicates ideas, or creates emotions — we are describing the outcome, not its nature.

A more interesting question is: how does design achieve all of this?

Neither art nor craft

Design borrows from art. It uses form, color, composition, rhythm, symbols, and metaphor. It also borrows from craft. It depends on technique, process, repetition, and discipline.

Yet design is neither art nor craft.

An artist can create without solving a problem. A craftsperson can produce something skillfully without communicating an idea. A designer is expected to do both — to create meaning through form.

Perhaps this is why design has always existed somewhere between art and craft. Not quite one. Not quite the other. And yet impossible without either.

Design is translation. A translation of human needs into forms, interfaces, objects, systems, and experiences.

The definition that feels closest to me is this. And the better we understand people, the better that translation becomes.
